Lab 08 · Key Distribution

BB84 Quantum Key Distribution

BB84 (Bennett & Brassard, 1984) lets Kumar and Tarun grow a shared secret key from single qubits sent over a quantum channel. Because any measurement disturbs a qubit in an unknown basis, an eavesdropper cannot intercept the channel without injecting errors — and the protocol reveals her presence. Run the simulation, with or without Eve, and watch the physics decide whether the key is safe.

Protocol Setup

01Prepare

Kumar picks a random bit and a random basis (Z or X), then prepares the qubit: |0⟩, |1⟩, or |+⟩/|−⟩ via H.

02Transmit

The qubit crosses the quantum channel. If Eve is on, she intercepts it, measures in a guessed basis, and resends the result.

03Measure

Tarun guesses a basis of his own. For an X-basis measurement he applies H first, then reads the qubit.

04Sift

Kumar and Tarun publicly compare bases — never the bits — and discard every qubit where the bases disagree.

05Verify

A sample of sifted bits is revealed to estimate the error rate: ≈0% means a clean channel, ≈25% means Eve was listening.

06Key

Within tolerance, the unrevealed sifted bits become the shared secret key. Otherwise the key is rejected.

ⓘ Why only bases are compared — the mechanism

1. The key bits travel as qubits, not as messages. Once Tarun measures, the carrier is consumed — nothing is left on the channel to steal.

2. Kumar and Tarun then talk over an ordinary public channel, but say only the basis used at each position (“Z”, “X”) — never the outcome bit.

3. A basis name alone is useless to Eve: knowing a qubit was measured in Z doesn't reveal whether the result was 0 or 1. The shared key forms silently at both ends.

05

Inferences & Conclusions

What the simulation teaches

Inferences

01

With Eve off, Kumar’s and Tarun’s sifted keys always agree — an ideal quantum channel gives a 0% error rate.

02

With Eve intercepting every qubit, the error rate clusters around 25%: she guesses the wrong basis half the time, and each wrong guess randomizes that qubit for Tarun half of the time (0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25).

03

Sifting discards about half of the transmitted qubits whether or not Eve is present — Kumar and Tarun choose bases independently, so only ~50% of positions ever match.

04

Longer transmissions make the signature clearer: at 32 qubits the ~25% eavesdropping rate is unmistakable, while a 4-qubit run can still be fooled by luck.

05

Security comes from measurement disturbance, not from hiding the channel — Eve is free to listen, but she cannot measure a qubit without leaving fingerprints in the error rate.

Conclusions

BB84 works because quantum measurement is never passive: observing a qubit in the wrong basis physically alters it. Kumar and Tarun deliberately sacrifice half of their qubits to sifting, and a sample of their key to verification — and in return they gain something no classical protocol can offer: proof, rooted in physics rather than computation, that no one listened in.

Nothing in this lab is scripted — every bit, basis and outcome is sampled live, so two runs with identical settings never match in detail, yet their statistics always converge: ≈0% errors on a clean channel, ≈25% with Eve. That statistical signature is exactly what real QKD systems look for, and it is why BB84 remains the foundation of quantum key distribution forty years on.
